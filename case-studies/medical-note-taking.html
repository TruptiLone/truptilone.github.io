AI-Powered Medical Note-Taking

Turning a consented doctor–patient conversation into a structured, clinician-approved visit note

1.Problem

Writing notes competes with the clinician’s attention during a visit. Notes are typed while the patient is talking, or written from memory after hours, which costs time and risks missing details the patient actually said.

2.Proposed solution

With the patient’s consent, the system records the conversation, transcribes it, works out who said what, and prepares an editable draft note. The clinician stays responsible for the record: nothing becomes final until they review and approve it. The design separates what AI is good at (transcribing and drafting) from what people must own (consent, clinical judgment and approval), and every automated step has a manual fallback.

3.System design

4.Design decision: a dedicated listening device

Audio is captured and handed off for processing by a dedicated in-room device rather than an existing clinic tablet. A separate device keeps concerns separate: it has one job, listening to the visit and sending the audio for processing, while the tablet stays free for charting and other work. A shared tablet may also be carried out of the room for other reasons, which would cut a recording short.

5.Challenge: telling the doctor and the patient apart

Speaker diarization splits the audio into anonymous voices (“Speaker A”, “Speaker B”) but cannot say which one is the doctor. Getting this wrong is serious: a patient’s “I think it’s my heart” could appear in the note as the doctor’s assessment.

  • Considered: separate microphones. A clip-on mic for the doctor and one for the patient makes each voice easy to label. It was rejected because managing a microphone for the patient during an in-person visit can become overwhelming.
  • Chosen: pre-configured voice profiles. The doctor and clinic staff (nurses, assistants) each record a short voice sample once. After diarization, each speaker is matched against these saved profiles: a match is labelled doctor or staff, and other voices are labelled patient, or caregiver, interpreter or unknown when unclear. Profiles are stored with staff consent.
  • Final review. The clinician sees each statement with its speaker during review and can correct a role, which updates the affected parts of the draft before approval.

Together, voice profiles and the clinician’s final review keep every statement attributed to the right person.
